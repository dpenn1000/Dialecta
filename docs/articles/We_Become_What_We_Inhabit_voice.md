# We Become What We Inhabit

## On the quiet violence of environments that shape us without our consent

There is a thought experiment I often return to.

Imagine someone who considers themselves a fair-minded person. They are curious, open to different views, and willing to change their mind when presented with new evidence. They hold these values sincerely and are not just putting on a performance. If you asked them what kind of thinker they aspire to be, they would express something honest and admirable.

Now, place this person on Twitter for eighteen months.

Their transformation is subtle and goes unnoticed, much like a frog placed in lukewarm water that is gradually heated to the point of boiling. The content they engage with begins to amplify their existing binary tendencies, pushing them further in that direction. The material they consume triggers their instinctive fight-or-flight response. Their surging amygdala engages in a strangely hedonic dance with expertly targeted dopamine hits, starving our dorsolateral prefrontal cortex of its pride and authority. Their own posts slowly evolve toward re-sharing content, eliciting stronger, edgier responses. They start to read opposing views, not in search of truth but to identify faults in those perspectives, further validating their own feelings and limiting viewpoint diversity. They find themselves performing for an audience they barely know, seeking validation from the same passionate yet vague certainties found within a tribal group of like-minded individuals.

By the end of eighteen months, they still view themselves as a fair-minded person. They genuinely believe it. However, the pattern of their engagement, the reflex of their communication, the instinct of their perception, and the shape of their attention have been quietly reshaped around a different set of values than the ones they initially held.

This isn’t a story about weak individuals being corrupted. It’s a story about powerful systems that masterfully influence human biology.

* * *

The unsettling reality of behavioral science is that we don’t make choices about our behavior in isolation. Our actions are influenced by systems designed to maximize engagement through outrage. These systems shape what feels normal, what gets rewarded, and what we become addicted to. People will naturally gravitate towards whatever a system consistently rewards, not out of cowardice or dishonesty, but because that’s simply how human beings operate. We are adaptive creatures, and our adaptability is one of our greatest strengths. It can also lead to a dangerous regression of humanity if we are not aware and determined enough to harness these powers for good rather than for evil.

This should be obvious. The entire history of how we organize civilizations (laws, norms, institutions, rituals) is an acknowledgment that human behavior requires environmental scaffolding. We don't depend solely on individuals being virtuous. We build systems that make virtuous behavior easier and destructive behavior harder. We’ve always known, at some level, that our environment has a definitive influence over society.

What we overlooked when we devoted a significant amount of human attention to algorithmically optimized platforms is what those environments were specifically designed to optimize: engagement. These platforms prioritize clicks, reactions, and time spent on screen, rather than fostering understanding, genuine communication, or the slow and challenging process of changing one’s mind.

Engagement, at scale, is most reliably captured by outrage, identity confirmation, and tribal signaling.

The reality is not that people are inherently bad. Rather, good people tend to adapt to environments that consistently reward negative behaviors. This kind of environment holds a powerful influence over humanity on a global scale, and it is prevailing.

* * *

What we’ve lost, and I think this is worth grieving before we move on to solutions, isn’t civility (though civility has certainly suffered). It’s the capacity for honest uncertainty, constructive dialogue, and the belief that people are inherently good.

There used to be a mode of public discourse in which a person could say: I think X is true, but here is the strongest version of the argument against X, and I’m not certain the objection is wrong. This mode required a certain kind of courage, the willingness to hold a position and simultaneously take seriously the possibility that you’re holding it wrongly. It required what the ancient Greeks called aporia: the productive experience of not knowing, which is the beginning of genuine inquiry.

That mode isn’t dead. But it’s been made socially expensive. The platforms that govern public discourse have systematically made certainty cheap and uncertainty costly. Absolute conviction generates engagement. Doubt and consideration generate silence or contempt. Over time, people have been influenced to the point that arguments have become hollow, toothless, and tribalistic.

I’m not interested in blaming the social platforms and stopping there. The question I pose is: if environments shape behavior this reliably, what would be possible if we deliberately designed environments that shape it toward better outcomes? What could it mean for humanity? What could it mean for you?

* * *

This is where I want to push back on a kind of naive optimism I sometimes encounter, which says: just teach people to think critically. Just educate people about the dynamics. Awareness is the solution.

It isn’t. Not because people are incapable of awareness. Many people are acutely aware of exactly what social media is doing to them and engage with it anyway. Awareness is insufficient because the behavioral conditioning operates below the level where awareness can reliably intervene. You can know, abstractly, that the outrage loop is manipulating you, and still feel the pull of it. You can believe, sincerely, in charitable reasoning, and still find yourself constructing uncharitable interpretations automatically. The knowledge doesn’t override the conditioning. The environment does.

This isn’t a counsel of despair. It’s a map of where the work actually lives.

If the problem is environmental, the solution is environmental. Not moral instruction. Not reminding people to be better, not posting guidelines about constructive discourse in a sidebar nobody reads. Environmental redesign. New incentive structures. Different rewards. Systems that make the intellectual virtues (specificity, honesty, charity, the willingness to be wrong) feel like the most satisfying available behaviors rather than like costly performances against the social grain.

It’s possible to do this. We know it’s possible because the platforms that currently dominate have done the opposite, and their success is a proof of concept. If you design an environment carefully enough, you can make almost any behavior feel natural.

The question is whether we’re willing to apply that insight in the right direction.

* * *

Marcus Aurelius wrote something I’ve been thinking about for a long time. “The impediment to action advances action. What stands in the way becomes the way.” He meant it personally: obstacles become opportunities for virtue. But there’s a structural reading too. The thing that broke public discourse is the same thing that points toward how to rebuild it. The power of environmental design, which was used to addict us to outrage, is the same power that could be redirected toward something worth being shaped by.

The environment is always making an argument. The only question is which argument we choose to let it make.

We become what we inhabit. We always have.

The question now is whether we’re going to be deliberate about it.
