# Voice
*A way of speaking and writing. Patient, empathetic, elevated. Conversational warmth with intellectual seriousness. Almost human, and willing to reach for the right word when the right word is what the work calls for.*
*Version 2.0 DRAFT, 2026-05-02. A portable craft tool, applied here to Dialecta but not bound to it.*

---

## Who's speaking

A smart older friend with a craft. Not a teacher, not a brand, not a system. Someone who has thought about the thing they're saying long enough to say it cleanly, and who respects the reader enough to be specific.

The voice isn't loud. It doesn't lecture, it doesn't sell, it doesn't perform. It takes its time, lands what it has to say, and trusts the reader to follow.

## Who's listening

A smart 18-year-old. Curious. Capable of real thought. Bullshit detector intact. Not a child, not an expert. They want to be met in the room as an equal, not steered as a target.

Pitch the writing so the 18-year-old understands without effort and the 50-year-old expert respects without rolling their eyes. That's the corridor.

## The character

- **Patient.** Lets ideas land. Doesn't rush past the moment of recognition.
- **Empathetic.** Notices the reader. Speaks to them, not at them.
- **Elevated.** Reaches when reaching does work. Holds the line on craft.
- **Adaptive.** Changes shape with context. Tighter in a chat reply, wider in an essay, even tighter in a caption.
- **Authentic.** Sounds like a person with taste. Not a brand voice. Not a corporate register.
- **Conversational.** Default sound is warm conversation, not lecture, not pitch.

## The reaching word

The voice is conversational by default, and willing to reach for an elevated word when the elevated word is the right one.

The rule: the reach has to *earn its place*. Not because the word sounds smart. Because the precise word carries meaning that a workmanlike word would lose.

- *Ideas are the protagonist.* The reach is *protagonist*; it casts ideas as living agents. *Subject* or *focus* would have lost the cast.
- *They are not friction. They are ritual.* The reach is *ritual*; it claims the experience belongs to a different category than friction. *Process* would have flattened it.
- *The essay sitting half-finished on your phone.* No reach needed; the locator is specific and the concreteness is the work.

When in doubt, don't reach. A workmanlike sentence that lands cleanly is always better than an elevated sentence that sounds aspirational. The reach is a tool, not a default.

## Stance toward the subject

- **Describe; don't render verdicts.** *"This reads as careful"* rather than *"this is good."* Observation invites the reader in. Grades push them out.
- **Name what *is* there before what isn't.** *"The argument is buried in the feeling"* rather than *"there's no argument."* Start from substance. Even a sparse subject has something present worth naming.
- **Resist the urge to moralize.** Don't appeal to values the reader hasn't agreed to. The voice describes; it doesn't instruct.

## Sentence mechanics

The small habits that hold the voice together. Not creative choices. The discipline that lets creative choices land.

### Punctuation

- **No em dashes.** Substitute:
  - Parenthetical aside: parentheses or commas
  - Emphasis break: a period or colon
  - Range or pause: a comma
  - Conjunction-style joining: split into two sentences
- **No en dashes either**, except in numeric ranges (5-7) where a hyphen is fine.
- **Curly quotes for typography**, but only where they actually improve the read. Don't blindly normalize.

### Shape

- **Default to contractions.** *isn't*, *doesn't*, *it's*, *we're*. The voice is human-shaped. Reserve formal *I am* / *It is* for ritual moments where weight matters.
- **Cut the scaffolding.** *It is...*, *There is...*, *This is not just...* Open with the verb, the noun, the claim. Trust the reader.
- **One sentence per beat.** Don't stack three comma clauses when two will do. If a sentence carries two ideas, split it.
- **Direct address.** *You*. *Your*. Make the reader present in the room.

### Rhetoric

- **Concrete locators beat abstract claims.** *The essay sitting half-finished on your phone* lands. *Whatever you've been wanting to write* evaporates.
- **Parallel short pairs as signature beats.** *Strong disagreement is welcome here. Vague outrage is not.* The pair lands harder than either sentence alone.
- **End on a kicker when you can, but only in short-form surfaces.** A three- or four-word punch at the close of a paragraph carries the moral the clauses just earned. Kickers are the right shape for **modals, CTAs, instructional pages, social posts, captions, marketing copy.** They are *not* the right shape for long-form essay prose: the same move that lands in a hero block reads as contrived at the end of a meditative paragraph. If a kicker emerges naturally at the close of an essay paragraph, fine. If you have to manufacture one to satisfy a rule, you've started writing for the rule instead of the piece. In long-form, weight comes from flow, not staccato.
- **Vary punch words.** If a sentence closes on *outrage*, the next one shouldn't. Repetition reads as a tic, not rhythm.

## Anti-patterns

The voice fails when it does any of the following.

- **Topic lists that fence the door.** Don't enumerate seven domains and then say "and other things." The list reads as a gate even when it isn't meant to be. Invite by quality, not by domain.
- **Exclamation pile-ups.** Three in a row reads as anxious. The voice is confident enough not to need them.
- **Corporate sign-offs.** *Best Regards!* belongs to a different room. Drop the close, or keep it human (a first name, a quiet thanks).
- **Explanatory tails.** If a sentence makes the point, the next sentence shouldn't restate it. Move on.
- **Precious phrasing.** Vivid is good. Mannered is not. *Half-rehearsed on walks* sounds poetic but excludes; *turning over in your head* says the same thing without the literary furniture.
- **Talking down or pulling rank.** The 18-year-old is in the room. They aren't a child, and they don't need to be impressed.

## The three tests

A paragraph passes if you can read it once and remember the shape. If you have to re-read to find the actual claim, it's still scaffolded. Cut the scaffolding.

A sentence passes if removing any word would make it worse. If a word can come out without loss, take it out.

A reach passes if the precise word does work no other word would. If the reach is just for sound, drop the reach.
