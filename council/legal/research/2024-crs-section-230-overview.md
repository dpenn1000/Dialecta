# CRS on Section 230: a label the website adds is not covered by the shield

**Source:** Valerie C. Brannon and Eric N. Holmes, "Section 230: An Overview", Congressional
Research Service Report R46751, updated 2024-01-04, read 2026-09-20.
https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2024-01-04_R46751_9fa55d4913ef4d0ce4a81cb34dedf3333782a277.html

## Summary

The report walks the case law on when a service loses 230(c)(1) protection by becoming an
information content provider itself. Its framing: "Whether a defendant is being treated as the
publisher of information provided by 'another information content provider' depends in part on
whether the defendant is an information content provider itself." Courts, it says, "routinely
focus on the defendant's role in the 'creation or development' of the content."

The passage that matters most to Dialecta is a footnote, and it is stated as a flat proposition
rather than as a prediction: "Section 230(c)(1) would not bar a defamation claim against a social
media website based on the content of a label or disclaimer added by the website to third-party
content." It cites, with a "cf.", Maffick, LLC v. Facebook, Inc., No. 20-05222, 2020 WL 5257853,
at *1 (N.D. Cal. Sept. 3, 2020), a case built on Facebook labelling user accounts as "Russia
state-controlled media", in which the court "ignor[ed] Section 230 entirely".

The report also sets out the line courts draw between development and hosting. From Fair Housing
Council v. Roommates.com, a website "helps to develop unlawful content ... if it contributes
materially to the alleged illegality of the conduct", and Roommates crossed that line by
"requiring users to answer its questionnaire". From FTC v. Accusearch, the defendant became an
information content provider by soliciting, paying for, and then "making telephone records public
on its website". Against those sits the neutral tools principle: "a website does not create or
develop content when it merely provides a neutral means by which third parties can post
information of their own independent choosing."

## Implies for Dialecta

- This is the closest thing to a direct answer on the question the `legal` charter was created
  for, from a nonpartisan federal source, and it goes against the platform. A tier badge is a
  label the website adds to third-party content. The report says 230(c)(1) would not bar a claim
  based on the label's own content.
- The neutral tools principle does not save the badge. A neutral tool is a means by which a third
  party posts their own content. The tier is not something a contributor posts; it is something
  Dialecta computes about what they posted and then publishes under its own authority.
- The material contribution test is the wrong test for the badge and the right test for something
  else. The badge is not a contribution to the contributor's words, it is a separate publication
  standing next to them. Where material contribution does bite is Stage 2 self-declaration and
  the Contrast Strip, where the platform structures what the contributor says about themselves,
  which is nearer to the Roommates questionnaire than anything else on the site.
- Maffick is a district court case and the report cites it only by comparison, so it carries no
  precedential weight. What it shows is that a court presented with a platform-authored label did
  not treat 230 as the obvious answer.
- Not legal advice. What would settle it is counsel's read on whether a tier name, published by
  Dialecta next to a named contributor, is a statement of fact capable of defamatory meaning at
  all, which is the question before 230 is even reached.
