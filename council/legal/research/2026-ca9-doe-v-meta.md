# Doe 1 v. Meta: the Ninth Circuit declines to follow Anderson, in Dialecta's own forum

**Source:** Doe 1 v. Meta Platforms, Inc., 2026 WL 1144707 (9th Cir. Apr. 28, 2026), read
2026-09-20 through Eric Goldman's Technology and Marketing Law Blog rather than the slip opinion.
Goldman is a law professor who has written on Section 230 for two decades and the blog is the
standard running commentary on the doctrine, but this is a secondary read of a recent decision
and the opinion should be read before anything load bearing rests on the quotations.
https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2026/05/ninth-circuit-panel-goes-out-of-its-way-to-question-section-230-doe-v-meta.htm

## Summary

Plaintiffs sued Meta over algorithmic amplification of anti-Rohingya content in Myanmar. The
Ninth Circuit upheld Section 230 protection. The panel held that "matching users with content is
publishing conduct" and that algorithmic recommendation is publisher activity within the shield
even where the algorithm is designed to maximise engagement.

The panel rejected the argument that an algorithm's output is the platform's own first-party
speech, characterising recommendations as conduct performed by a publisher upon third-party
content rather than as the platform's own message. It distinguished Anderson v. TikTok on that
basis and cited Dyroff v. Ultimate Software approvingly.

Goldman's own framing is that the panel went out of its way to question Section 230 in other
respects, so the decision is not a straightforward endorsement of a broad shield. On the
algorithmic question specifically it lands where the pre-2024 consensus did.

## Implies for Dialecta

- **This is the controlling forum and it goes the other way from Anderson.** Dialecta is operated
  from Arizona, which is in the Ninth Circuit. The earlier filing at
  `council/legal/research/2024-ca3-anderson-v-tiktok.md` treated the Second Circuit's Force v.
  Facebook as the relevant counterweight; the relevant authorities are actually Dyroff, Doe 1 v.
  Meta and, in the other direction, Fair Housing Council v. Roommates.com.
- **The algorithmic theory against Dialecta is now weak here, and it was never the strong one.**
  Anderson supported an argument that the classifier's operation is the platform's expressive
  activity. In the Ninth Circuit that argument runs into a decision from five months ago that
  rejects it directly. The tier label position should stop leaning on Anderson.
- **The label theory is untouched by this.** Doe 1 v. Meta is about selecting and arranging other
  people's content. It says nothing about a platform publishing a sentence it wrote. The CRS
  statement that Section 230(c)(1) does not bar a claim based on the content of a label a website
  adds survives intact, and it was always the better argument. The correction narrows the case
  against Dialecta rather than removing it.
- **Roommates.com is the Ninth Circuit authority that cuts the other way, and it is en banc.**
  The material contribution test lives here as binding law. It bears on Stage 2 self-declaration
  and the Contrast Strip, where the platform structures what a contributor says about themselves,
  which is nearer to the Roommates questionnaire than anything else on the site.
- **Net effect on confidence.** Section 230 not shielding the authored label stays high. The risk
  that a court treats the classifier's operation itself as first-party speech drops from a live
  appellate theory to a minority position the home circuit has recently declined.
- Not legal advice, and read at second hand. Reading the slip opinion is now the highest value
  item on the reading list.
