# Anderson v. TikTok: a platform's own output is first-party speech and Section 230 does not reach it

**Source:** Anderson v. TikTok, Inc., No. 22-3061 (3d Cir. Aug. 27, 2024) (precedential), Shwartz,
J., read 2026-09-20 from the court's own slip opinion PDF.
https://www2.ca3.uscourts.gov/opinarch/223061p.pdf

## Summary

TikTok's For You Page algorithm recommended a "Blackout Challenge" video to a ten year old who
died attempting it. The district court dismissed on Section 230. The Third Circuit reversed in
part, and the sentence that carries the opinion is this: "ICSs are immunized only if they are
sued for someone else's expressive activity or content (i.e., third-party speech), but they are
not immunized if they are sued for their own expressive activity or content (i.e., first-party
speech)."

The court got there through Moody v. NetChoice, LLC, 144 S. Ct. 2383 (2024). It read NetChoice as
holding that a platform algorithm reflecting "editorial judgments" about "compiling the
third-party speech it wants in the way it wants" is the platform's own "expressive product". It
then took the step the Supreme Court had not: "Given the Supreme Court's observations that
platforms engage in protected first-party speech under the First Amendment when they curate
compilations of others' content via their expressive algorithms, it follows that doing so amounts
to first-party speech under Section 230, too."

The opinion quotes NetChoice describing what platforms do to uploaded posts: they "will have
removed some content entirely; ranked or otherwise prioritized what remains; and sometimes added
warnings or labels", and by doing so they "shape other parties' expression into their own curated
speech products".

Two limits are written into the opinion. Footnote 12: "We reach this conclusion specifically
because TikTok's promotion of a Blackout Challenge video on Nylah's FYP was not contingent upon
any specific user input. Had Nylah viewed a Blackout Challenge video through TikTok's search
function ... TikTok may be viewed more like a repository of third-party content than an
affirmative promoter of such content." Footnote 13 concedes the holding "may be in tension with"
the Third Circuit's own Green v. America Online and "may depart from the pre-NetChoice views of
other circuits", then lists them: Dyroff (9th Cir.), Force v. Facebook (2d Cir.), Jane Doe No. 1
v. Backpage.com (1st Cir.), Jones v. Dirty World (6th Cir.), Klayman (D.C. Cir.), Johnson v.
Arden (8th Cir.), Doe v. MySpace (5th Cir.).

## Implies for Dialecta

- This is the strongest appellate support for treating Dialecta's classifier output as the
  platform's own speech, and it is stronger for Dialecta than it was for TikTok. TikTok's
  first-party speech was an arrangement of other people's videos. Dialecta's tier badge and
  commenter message are new sentences the platform composed. If selection and ranking is
  expressive activity, authored text is comfortably so.
- The NetChoice language the Third Circuit relied on names labels directly: platforms "sometimes
  added warnings or labels" and thereby "shape other parties' expression into their own curated
  speech products". The AI Classification Card and the Contrast Strip are exactly that move.
- Footnote 12 is the fact that would move the case, and it cuts Dialecta's way nowhere. The
  distinction it draws is between a platform that promotes without user input and a repository
  that answers a user's search. Classification is not contingent on any reader's input at all; it
  fires on submission, every time, automatically.
- Footnote 13 is the honest counterweight. Seven circuits went the other way before NetChoice,
  and Dialecta is not in the Third Circuit. A Connecticut operator sits in the Second Circuit,
  whose Force v. Facebook is on the list of decisions Anderson departs from. That materially
  weakens the case against Dialecta in its own forum, and it is the single most important fact
  for anyone reading this note.
- Anderson is a products liability and negligence case, not defamation, and the claim was that
  the algorithm was defectively designed. Nothing in it decides what a label says about a person.
- Not legal advice. What would settle it is counsel's read on how the Second Circuit is likely to
  treat Force v. Facebook after NetChoice, which is a question about the direction of a circuit,
  not a question a non-lawyer can answer from reading opinions.
