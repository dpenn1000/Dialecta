# Milkovich v. Lorain Journal: there is no opinion label that saves a statement

**Source:** Milkovich v. Lorain Journal Co., 497 U.S. 1 (1990), read 2026-09-20 via Cornell Legal
Information Institute. https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/497/1

## Summary

The Court refused to recognise a separate constitutional privilege for anything labelled opinion:
"We do not think this passage from Gertz was intended to create a wholesale defamation exemption
for anything that might be labeled 'opinion.'"

The reason is that opinion language does not remove a factual implication. "Simply couching such
statements in terms of opinion does not dispel these implications; and the statement, 'In my
opinion Jones is a liar,' can cause as much damage to reputation as the statement, 'Jones is a
liar.'" The mechanism is stated directly: "If a speaker says, 'In my opinion John Jones is a
liar,' he implies a knowledge of facts which lead to the conclusion that Jones told an untruth."

What does protect a statement is that it cannot be shown false. "A statement on matters of public
concern must be provable as false before there can be liability under state defamation law."

Arizona applies the same test. Turner v. Devlin, 174 Ariz. 201, 205 (1993), holds that opinions
are non-defamatory where they state matters "not susceptible to proof of truth or falsity" and
that "cannot reasonably be interpreted as actual facts". Arizona's elements for a private figure
are a defamatory false statement of fact about the plaintiff, publication to a third party, fault
at least amounting to negligence, and harm.

## Implies for Dialecta

- **A disclaimer is worth close to nothing, and this is the correction to make whenever someone
  says "every platform has disclaimers".** Adding "this is our system's opinion" to a tier badge
  does not convert it into opinion. Milkovich is the case that closed that route thirty-six years
  ago.
- **What protects the badge is that the facts are on the page, not that the badge is called an
  opinion.** The danger Milkovich identifies is the implication of *undisclosed* facts. The AI
  Classification Card publishes claim, specificity, emotion, tribal markers, article engagement
  and opposing view engaged, beside the comment itself. A reader with all of that in front of
  them is not being asked to trust an undisclosed fact. That is the entire defence, and it is a
  build constraint rather than a copy choice.
- **"Provable as false" is the test each tier should be read against, and the tiers do not all
  score the same.** Fog means a reader cannot tell what the commenter believes, which is close to
  unprovable. Heat means emotion without a specific claim, also hard to falsify. Breach means
  content "targets a person, not an idea", which is much closer to a checkable assertion about
  what a person did, and it is published beside a named account with the original text withheld
  so no reader can check it. Breach is the tier where Milkovich bites hardest, and the withheld
  text is what makes it bite.
- **The Contrast Strip is the same problem in a different place.** "Commenter declared Forum.
  Engine read Stance." is provable or disprovable only against a rubric, and it is published
  permanently as a statement about a person's self-assessment rather than about a comment.
- **Fault at negligence is the standard, because contributors are private individuals.** No
  actual malice hurdle protects Dialecta here, which is another reason the disclosed-basis
  defence carries the weight rather than the fault element.
- Not legal advice. What would need counsel: whether the Breach notice, published without the
  text it describes, is "provable as false" under Turner v. Devlin. That is the single sharpest
  question in this tree and suppressing the text is what creates it.
