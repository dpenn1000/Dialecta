# Yetman v. English: Arizona's fact/opinion line for defamation

**Source:** Yetman v. English, 168 Ariz. 71, 811 P.2d 323 (Ariz. 1991), the Arizona Supreme
Court's controlling statement of the opinion privilege. Read 2026-09-20 through case summaries and
holding excerpts at Justia, CourtListener, and Casemine, cross-checked against secondary
descriptions at the State Bar of Arizona's Recommended Arizona Jury Instructions on defamation and

## Summary, at the confidence the sourcing above supports

David Yetman, an elected county supervisor, sued William English, a state legislator, over a
remark English made at a Republican Club luncheon calling Yetman "a communist" in connection with
a zoning dispute. The Arizona Supreme Court held that Article 2, Section 6 of the Arizona
Constitution provides no greater privilege for defamatory statements than the First Amendment;
Arizona does not have an enhanced state constitutional speech privilege for defamation the way
some states do. The court rejected English's claim that his remark was absolutely protected as a
matter of law, and remanded for a jury determination of whether the average listener would have
understood the remark to state or imply actual facts about Yetman rather than pure rhetorical
hyperbole or opinion.

Multiple secondary sources converge on the same summary of Arizona's operating test, which they
attribute to this line of authority: courts ask whether the average reader or listener would
understand a statement to imply a provably false factual assertion, judged from the totality of
the circumstances (the statement's context, the forum, and its phrasing), and "only in the
clearest cases" will a court take that question away from a jury and decide it as a matter of law
that a statement states or implies actual fact and is therefore unprotected. This is the same
family of test as Milkovich v. Lorain Journal Co., 497 U.S. 1 (1990), which the sources say Arizona
has expressly adopted, and it is structurally the same test this tree already uses for the federal
baseline (`council/legal/positions/2026-09-20-tier-label-first-party-speech.md`).

## Implies for Dialecta

- Arizona does not appear to hold a stronger or weaker fact/opinion line than the general First
  Amendment baseline this tree has already been arguing from. Nothing found here overturns the
  tier-label position's existing reasoning that a tier badge, published beside its own disclosed
  basis, reads as the platform's evaluative characterisation rather than a bare factual accusation.
  If anything, a rubric published beside the label is exactly the kind of context Yetman's
  totality-of-circumstances framing rewards, because it gives the average reader the material to
  see the label as the platform's own inference rather than as an assertion of an underlying fact.
- The jury-question default cuts against Dialecta at the margin. Yetman's holding is that courts
  rarely resolve the fact/opinion line themselves; the ordinary path is a jury deciding what the
  average reader would have understood. That means the disclosed-basis defence is a strong
  argument to make to a jury, not a reliable basis for an early dismissal on the pleadings, and it
  is a large part of why the anti-SLAPP finding in `2026-az-anti-slapp-12-751.md` matters as much
  as it does: it is a separate, faster route to the same early exit that the opinion privilege
  alone does not obviously provide.
- This does not resolve legal-02's question one. It narrows it from "no state authority exists on
  this at all" to "Arizona's general framework is known and looks ordinary; applying it to the
  specific words a tier badge and its Classification Card use is still a fact-bound question a
  non-lawyer should not finish." That is real progress against a position that was previously
  `(unsourced)`, and it is still not an answer.
- Not legal advice. What would settle it: a licensed Arizona attorney's read of an actual rendered

  RAJI defamation instruction rather than the secondary summaries this note relies on.
