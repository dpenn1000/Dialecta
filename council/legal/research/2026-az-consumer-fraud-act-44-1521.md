# The Arizona Consumer Fraud Act, and the clause that imports FTC deception law into state law

**Source:** A.R.S. Sections 44-1521 to 44-1534, read 2026-09-20 through FindLaw's reproduction of
Section 44-1522 and through three Arizona practitioner summaries. The statutory quotations are
from FindLaw; the private-right-of-action and limitations points are from the practitioner
summaries and were not traced to the Arizona cases that establish them.
https://codes.findlaw.com/az/title-44-trade-and-commerce/az-rev-st-sect-44-1522/

## Summary

Section 44-1522(A) makes it unlawful to use "deception, deceptive or unfair act or practice,
fraud, false pretense, false promise, misrepresentation, or concealment, suppression or omission
of any material fact with intent that others rely on such concealment, suppression or omission, in
connection with the sale or advertisement of any merchandise", whether or not anyone is actually
misled.

**The clause that matters most here is 44-1522(C).** The legislature directed that in construing
subsection A, "the courts may use as a guide interpretations given by the federal trade commission
and the federal courts to 15 United States Code Sections 45, 52 and 55(a)(1)." Arizona deception
law is therefore read against FTC Act Section 5, by the statute's own instruction.

**Private right of action.** Implied by Arizona case law rather than stated on the statute's face.
A private plaintiff may sue.

**Limitations.** One year, running from discovery of the deceptive act rather than from the act.

**Public enforcement.** The Attorney General, with civil penalties and restitution.

Two features worth holding onto: "false promise" is named in the statute alongside
misrepresentation, and an omission is actionable only where there was intent that others rely on
it.

## Implies for Dialecta

- **This is the statute a founding-price promise actually lives under, not a privacy law and not
  Section 230.** Arizona has no comprehensive consumer privacy act
  (`research/2026-az-no-comprehensive-privacy-law.md`), and the Consumer Fraud Act is what fills
  the space where a paid membership is concerned.
- **44-1522(C) collapses two questions into one.** Anything that would be deceptive under FTC Act
  Section 5 is the thing an Arizona court is told to use as its guide. So the FTC deception
  standard, a representation or omission likely to mislead a reasonable consumer, material to the
  decision, is the working test for whether "locked founding price" or "permanent badge" is safe
  to say.
- **"False promise" reaches a promise the promisor cannot or does not intend to keep at the time
  it is made.** The exposure in a founding-cohort pitch is not the price. It is promising a
  capability that does not exist yet on a date nobody controls.
- **A one-year limitations period, running from discovery, is short in the operator's favour and
  long in his disfavour.** Short, because most complaints about a membership surface in the first
  renewal cycle. Long, because a promise that quietly fails is not discovered until someone looks.
- **The realistic scale is one annoyed member in an Arizona justice court, not an AG action.** A
  hundred people at $50 is $5,000 of revenue, which does not attract a regulator. It does support
  a small claim, and the cheap control is the same either way: say the conditions before the money
  moves, and keep the record of what was said.
- Not legal advice. Neither the private right of action nor the limitations period was traced to
  the Arizona decisions that establish them, and both are stated here on secondary authority.
