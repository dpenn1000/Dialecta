# Douglas v. Talk America: you cannot change the deal by editing the page

**Source:** Douglas v. U.S. District Court for the Central District of California ex rel. Talk
America, Inc., 495 F.3d 1062 (9th Cir. 2007) (per curiam), read 2026-09-20 through FindLaw's
reproduction and Eric Goldman's contemporaneous write-up rather than the slip opinion.
https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-9th-circuit/1307125.html
https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2007/07/ninth_circuit_s_1.htm

## Summary

Talk America acquired AOL's long-distance customers and later added four terms to the service
contract: new charges, a class action waiver, an arbitration clause and a New York choice of law.
It posted the revised contract on its website and, on the plaintiff's account, never told him. He
kept using the service for four years.

The Ninth Circuit held that a party cannot unilaterally change the terms of a contract and must
obtain the other party's consent. A revised contract is an offer, and an offer does not bind until
it is accepted. An offeree cannot accept terms he does not know about, so continued use of a
service is not acceptance where the user had no notice that new terms were proposed.

## Implies for Dialecta

- **Binding in Dialecta's circuit.** Arizona is in the Ninth. This is not persuasive authority,
  it is the rule.
- **It decides the timing question on the Charter badge.** Today nobody has paid and no contract
  exists, so a condition published now is a term of the offer. After the first payment against
  "permanent badge, locked founding price", adding a condition by editing the terms page is an
  offer nobody accepted, and continued membership does not accept it.
- **It cuts both ways and the second way is the useful one.** A reservation-of-rights clause
  saying the operator may change terms at any time does not rescue an unnoticed change, and a
  clause broad enough to allow anything risks being read as illusory. The thing that works is
  notice plus a real choice, which is also what the state renewal statutes require for a fee
  change.
- **The control is cheap and it is a habit rather than a document.** Any change to a paying
  member's terms goes out as a direct notice with a date and a cancellation path, and the notice
  is kept. That is the same email the California and New York statutes already want.
- Not legal advice. Read at second hand. What would need counsel: whether a founding-cohort price
  promise is a contract term enforceable indefinitely or a revocable policy, which is the question
  underneath everything above.
