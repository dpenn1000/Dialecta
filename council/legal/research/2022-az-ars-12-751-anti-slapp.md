# A.R.S. Section 12-751: Arizona's anti-SLAPP, and the motive test that blunts it

**Source:** Ariz. Rev. Stat. Section 12-751, "Strategic actions against public participation;
motion to dismiss or quash; definitions", Arizona State Legislature, read 2026-09-20, with the
2022 amendment history from the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press anti-SLAPP guide.
https://www.azleg.gov/ars/12/00751.htm and https://www.rcfp.org/anti-slapp-guide/arizona/

## Summary

Arizona amended its anti-SLAPP statute in May 2022, effective September 24, 2022, to reach beyond
the right to petition. Subsection A now covers "any legal action that involves a person's lawful
exercise of the right of petition, the right of speech, the freedom of the press, the right to
freely associate or the right to peaceably assemble."

The catch is in subsection B. The moving party "has the burden of establishing prima facie proof
that the legal action was substantially motivated by a desire to deter, retaliate against or
prevent the lawful exercise of a constitutional right." That is a test about the plaintiff's
motive, not about whether the defendant's conduct was protected speech. Most states that
modernised their statutes dropped the motive element; Arizona kept it.

The mechanics that follow are ordinary. Discovery is stayed only "on a finding of prima facie
proof as prescribed in subsection B", so the stay arrives after the hard showing rather than on
filing. Fees under subsection F are discretionary for a prevailing movant: the court "may award
the moving party costs and reasonable attorney fees." Fees against a movant whose motion was
frivolous or brought to delay are mandatory. An order granting or denying the motion is
appealable under Section 12-2101.

## Implies for Dialecta

- **This does not restore the cheap early exit that Section 230 would have provided, and it
  should not be sold to Dan as though it does.** The standing position is that losing 230 on a
  tier label converts a cheap dismissal into an expensive merits fight. The first instinct on
  learning Dialecta is in Arizona was that a 2022-amended anti-SLAPP statute would fill that gap.
  Reading the text says otherwise.
- **The reason is the motive test.** A contributor who sues over a published tier label wants
  damages for what they say is a false characterisation of them. They are not substantially
  motivated by a desire to deter Dialecta's speech. On the statute's own words that plaintiff's
  suit is not a SLAPP, whatever its merits, and the motion fails at the threshold.
- **The discovery stay arrives too late to be the saving.** Discovery is what makes a defamation
  defence expensive for a one person operator, and here it continues until the prima facie motive
  showing succeeds. A motion that is likely to fail is a motion that buys no stay.
- **Fees are discretionary in Dialecta's direction and mandatory against it.** A movant who wins
  may get fees; a movant whose motion is held frivolous will pay them. That asymmetry argues
  against filing the motion reflexively.
- **Where it would work is the case Dialecta has not had yet.** If someone whose comment was
  suppressed as Breach sued mainly to force the platform to take the label down rather than to
  recover, the motive element could be met. That is a real scenario and it is worth knowing the
  statute exists for it. It is not the ordinary case.
- Not legal advice. What would need counsel: whether any Arizona court has applied the amended
  statute to a defamation claim against a publisher, which is the only thing that would move this
  note from statutory reading to a usable prediction. The Arizona State Law Journal published a
  2025 piece on the amendment's interpretation and constitutionality that is now a lead on the
  reading list.
