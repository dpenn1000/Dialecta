# Arizona's anti-SLAPP statute, expanded 2022: A.R.S. Sections 12-751 and 12-752

**Source:** A.R.S. Section 12-751, primary enrolled text, Arizona State Legislature, read
2026-09-20. https://www.azleg.gov/ars/12/00751.htm. Section 12-752's own page returned a 404 on
this attempt; its fee-shifting text below is drawn from convergent secondary description across
the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press, the Digital Media Law Project, a practitioner
summary (Kolsrud Law, marked as such, a firm with a litigation interest in the statute), and an
Arizona State Law Journal student blog post dated 2025-11-09 that engaged the primary text
directly and is the strongest secondary source of the group. Section 12-751 itself is primary and
fully read.

## Summary

Arizona rewrote its anti-SLAPP law in May 2022, effective September 24, 2022. Before the
amendment, it protected only speech petitioning government. As amended, Section 12-751 protects
"any lawful exercise of the right of petition, the right of speech, the freedom of the press, the
right to freely associate or the right to peaceably assemble" under the United States or Arizona
Constitution, with no requirement that the speech concern a matter of public interest or public
concern. That is a broader trigger than several states' versions, which the law journal source
notes explicitly by contrast to California's.

A defendant moves to dismiss or quash within sixty days of service (longer at the court's
discretion), and must make prima facie proof that the action "was substantially motivated by a
desire to deter, retaliate against or prevent the lawful exercise of a constitutional right." On
that showing, discovery stays automatically until the motion is decided, subject to a good-cause
exception, and the court must grant the motion unless the responding party shows the action is
justified: by "clearly established law" if the plaintiff is a state actor, or by "existing law or a
reasonable argument for extending or modifying existing law" if not. Fee-shifting under Section
12-752, per the secondary sources, is no longer automatic for a prevailing movant since the 2022
rewrite made it discretionary ("may"), except that fees remain mandatory in favour of a movant who
defeats a state actor's suit; fees shift mandatorily to a prevailing plaintiff where the motion was
frivolous or filed solely to delay.

The Arizona State Law Journal source, current as of November 2025, records a live constitutional
challenge: the Maricopa County Attorney's Office filed a special action in October 2024 arguing the
2022 expansion violates separation of powers, conflicts with Arizona's Victims' Bill of Rights,
conflicts with procedural court rules, and is unconstitutionally vague and overbroad. That
challenge arose in a criminal prosecution context, which is a different posture from a civil
defamation suit against Dialecta, but it means the statute's current breadth should be treated as
live rather than settled.

## Implies for Dialecta

- This is the most consequential single finding of this research pass and it was not something
  the Connecticut-framed tree had any occasion to look for, because no equivalent search was ever
  run for Connecticut's own anti-SLAPP statute, Conn. Gen. Stat. Section 52-196a. That gap predates
  the jurisdiction error and is not fixed by this note; it is named so the gap is visible rather
  than quietly carried forward.
- If Dialecta is sued over a published tier badge, Contrast Strip entry, or Breach notice, and
  Dialecta is the moving party invoking its own right of speech or freedom of the press in
  publishing those surfaces, Section 12-751 is a second, faster route to an early exit than the
  opinion-privilege argument in `1991-az-yetman-v-english-opinion-privilege.md`. Where that
  argument is a jury question under Yetman's own default, an anti-SLAPP motion is decided by the
  court on the pleadings and affidavits, with discovery stayed while it is pending. That materially
  changes the "losing Section 230 is a defence-cost exposure" calculus in
  `positions/2026-09-20-tier-label-first-party-speech.md`: some of that defence cost may be
  recoverable in Arizona in a way this tree has not priced anywhere.
- The no-public-concern-requirement breadth is a genuine asset here, because it means the motion
  does not turn on convincing a court that a tier badge about one contributor's comment is a matter
  of public concern, a fight Dialecta might lose given how small and specific the labelled unit is.
- The live constitutional challenge is a reason to hold this as a real but not fully secured
  advantage, not to promise it in an argument to Dan as though it were settled law. Watch it rather
  than build on it as bedrock.
- Not legal advice. What would settle it: whether a suit against Dialecta would actually be
  venued in Arizona at all, which is a personal-jurisdiction question this tree has not resolved
  either (see the correction in `positions/2026-09-20-tier-label-first-party-speech.md`), and a
  primary read of Section 12-752's text rather than the secondary convergence this note relies on.
