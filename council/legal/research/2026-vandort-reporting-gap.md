# Van Dort on the reporting gap: no US law makes a platform report a credible threat

**Source:** Heather Van Dort, "Closing the Reporting Gap: Building a Legal Framework for
Reporting Serious Online Threats", Minnesota Journal of Law, Science and Technology, 2026-03-24,
read 2026-09-20. A student staff post on a law journal's blog, so persuasive rather than
authoritative, and read for the proposition it sources rather than for its conclusion.
https://journals.law.umn.edu/mjlst/2026/03/24/closing-the-reporting-gap-building-a-legal-framework-for-reporting-serious-online-threats

## Summary

The article's central factual claim is stated flatly: "there is nothing in the Act, nor any other
U.S. law, which requires providers to disclose credible, serious threats to law enforcement." Its
argument is that this is a gap that ought to be closed, which means the author has every
incentive to state the current baseline accurately and no incentive to overstate it.

It describes the voluntary route that exists instead. The Stored Communications Act permits a
provider to disclose customer data where it "in good faith, believes that an emergency involving
danger of death or serious physical injury to any person requires disclosure without delay". The
consequence the article objects to is that "providers are left to their own discretion to decide
when user communications on their platforms are sufficiently concerning to justify reporting to
law enforcement".

## Implies for Dialecta

- Dialecta owes no duty to report a threat. The Breach tier can fire, the comment can be
  suppressed, and nothing in law requires the platform to tell anyone. That is the baseline, and
  the charter's concern about a Breach that "names a threat and routes it nowhere" is a concern
  about ethics and about the person being threatened, not about liability.
- The absence of a duty is not the absence of a decision. Discretion means the choice will be
  made by whoever is awake when it happens, under pressure, unless it is made in advance. Writing
  the routing rule now costs a paragraph; writing it during an incident costs judgment.
- The emergency exception is the mechanism to write that paragraph around: danger of death or
  serious physical injury, good faith belief, disclosure without delay. Those words give the
  standard a rule can be written to, and they are narrower than what a Breach classification
  detects. Most Breach comments will be insults, not emergencies, and a rule that treats them the
  same will either over-report or become dead letter.
- One duty does exist and is sourced separately at
  `council/legal/research/2026-usc-2258a-csam-reporting.md`. Nothing here contradicts it; that
  duty is about child sexual abuse material and not about threats.
- Not legal advice. This is a student post, and the proposition it supports is a negative, which
  is the hardest kind of claim to verify from a single source. What would settle it: counsel
  confirming there is no Connecticut reporting statute that reaches an online publisher, which is
  a state law question this national-scope article does not answer.
