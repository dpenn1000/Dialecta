# Effort for Payment: A Tale of Two Markets

**Source:** Heyman, J., and Ariely, D. (2004). "Effort for Payment: A Tale of Two Markets." Psychological Science, 15(11), 787-793. DOI 10.1111/j.0956-7976.2004.00757.x https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.0956-7976.2004.00757.x

## Summary

Building on Fiske's (1992) relational-models theory, the authors distinguish two frames a person can apply to the same exchange of effort: a social market (governed by social norms, not sensitive to the size of compensation, sometimes producing more effort for no pay than for low pay) and a monetary market (governed by market norms, sharply sensitive to the size of compensation). Across five experiments, introducing any monetary payment, even a small one, was enough to switch participants from the social frame to the market frame; a non-monetary gift of equivalent value (candy) did not trigger the switch. The frame, not the amount, is what changes. Once in a market frame, people evaluate the exchange as a transaction: they compare effort given to payment received and adjust accordingly.

## Implies for Dialecta

- Directly answers the mechanism half of `treasurer`'s blindspot (`exchange/open/2026-09-19-002-blindspot-membership-intrinsic-motivation.md`): paying for a Dialecta membership, even $50 a year, is itself the event that risks moving a contributor from a social frame ("I write because the discourse matters") to a market frame ("I paid, so I evaluate what I get for it"). The size of the fee is not the lever. The presence of a price is.
- This sharpens rather than confirms the treasurer's fear. It is not a general claim that paying members misbehave; it is a specific claim that a price tag changes which norms a person applies to the relationship, and market norms are the ones under which a person totals up value received, including whether their comment was tiered the way they expected.
- Names the surface precisely: Stage 3 community voting and nomination is the place a market frame would show up, because it is the one place a paying member's dissatisfaction becomes an action inside the product rather than a feeling about it. It does not implicate the classification engine itself, which never sees payment status.
- The mitigation this implies is structural, not persuasive: keep membership and classification mechanically separate (no signal of paid status reaches the classifier, the vote weight, or the nomination panel), so there is no channel for a market-frame expectation to act on even if a member holds one. That is a cheaper and more durable answer than trying to talk members out of the frame once they are in it.
