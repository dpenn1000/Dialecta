# Connecticut Data Privacy Act: who it covers, and the 2026 threshold rewrite

**Source:** Connecticut General Assembly, "Connecticut General Statutes Sections 42-515 to 42-525: Data Privacy and Security", Conn. Gen. Stat. Section 42-516 (Applicability), as amended by Section 6 of Public Act 25-113, effective July 1, 2026, read 2026-09-20. https://law.justia.com/codes/connecticut/title-42/chapter-743jj/section-42-516/

## Summary

The Connecticut Data Privacy Act (CTDPA), Conn. Gen. Stat. Sections 42-515 to 42-525, took effect July 1, 2023. Its applicability test changed completely on July 1, 2026 under Public Act 25-113 (Senate Bill 1295, signed by Governor Ned Lamont on June 24, 2025), and that new test is already in force as of this note's read date. A direct fetch of Justia's and the Connecticut General Assembly's own pages for the amended section returned an access error in both cases; the amended text quoted below is drawn from a search index of Justia's codified text and cross-checked against Snell and Wilmer, Benesch, and WilmerHale client alerts describing the same amendment.

The original text (July 2023 through June 30, 2026) applied to a person conducting business in Connecticut, or targeting Connecticut residents, that during the preceding calendar year either controlled or processed the personal data of "not less than one hundred thousand consumers," excluding data processed solely to complete a payment transaction, or controlled or processed the data of "not less than twenty-five thousand consumers" and "derived more than twenty-five per cent of their gross revenue from the sale of personal data."

As amended, Section 42-516 applies to a person conducting business in the state, or targeting products or services to state residents, that during the preceding calendar year meets any of three tests: controlled or processed the personal data of "not fewer than thirty-five thousand consumers," excluding payment-only data; "control[s] or process[es] consumers' sensitive data," also excluding payment-only data; or "offer[s] consumers' personal data for sale in trade or commerce." The amendment deleted the old 25,000-consumer, 25-percent-revenue test rather than layering the new triggers on top of it, and put no consumer-count floor on the sensitive-data or sale triggers at all: a business processing even one resident's sensitive data, or selling personal data in any amount, is covered regardless of size. Counsel commentary reports the amendment also expanded the statutory definition of sensitive data (adding disability and treatment status, transgender and nonbinary status, neural data, and financial account information to the original categories: race or ethnicity, religion, health diagnosis, sexual orientation, immigration status, genetic or biometric data, precise geolocation, and children's data); this note has not independently verified that full list against primary text.

## Implies for Dialecta

- Fourteen members is far under the new 35,000-consumer general threshold, but that threshold is no longer the only door in. If the AI fingerprint or any future field touches a category the amended statute treats as sensitive data, CTDPA can apply at any size, because that trigger carries no consumer-count floor.
- Whether Dialecta's opinion-position tagging or AI-generated archetype counts as "sensitive data" under the current statutory list is exactly the kind of line a non-lawyer should not draw alone. Not legal advice: what would settle it is a Connecticut attorney's read of Dialecta's actual classification categories against the current Section 42-515 definition of sensitive data, not the older category list secondary sources still quote from 2023.
- A plain search on CTDPA thresholds surfaces the pre-July-2026 100,000 and 25,000 figures well after they stopped controlling, the same trap this note's own sourcing ran into. Practice: pull the current codified text or the enrolled Public Act before quoting a threshold on a live compliance question.
- Section 42-520(a)(1)'s minimization duty, filed as its own note, is a reason to scope the AI fingerprint feature to what its stated purpose needs before the applicability question above is even resolved, since a narrower feature is a smaller target under either version of Section 42-516.
