Context

The Problem These Tools Solve

Why a binary agree/disagree button is the wrong unit of measurement for genuine discourse.

Binary voting collapses the actual structure of opinion. On almost any substantive question, people do not simply agree or disagree. They weigh competing values differently. They share conclusions but hold entirely different reasons. They occupy positions that are orthogonal to the framing the article offers. A thumbs-up or thumbs-down captures none of that.

Opinion mapping tools replace the binary with a spatial representation. The reader places themselves in a landscape rather than choosing a side. The aggregate of all placements becomes a map of where the community actually stands, which is often more interesting than the article itself. A tight cluster around one pole tells a different story than a community split into two camps, which tells a different story again from a community evenly distributed across the space.

The goal is not to produce a score or a vote count. The goal is to produce a picture. Positions as landscapes, not scorecards.

Core design principle: The tool should feel like reflection, not polling. The framing of each axis and the copy surrounding the interaction matter enormously. A reader should finish placing their marker feeling that the tool understood them, not that it filed them.

Tool 01 of 04

The 2-Axis Cartesian Plot

The launch default. Two independent dimensions, one placed dot, one heatmap of the community.

Launch Default
Tool 01
2-Axis Cartesian Plot
A dot on a plane. The simplest meaningful spatial representation of opinion.
How It Works

Two axes define two independent tensions in the debate. The reader clicks or taps to place a single dot anywhere in the 2D space. Their position expresses both dimensions simultaneously. The aggregate of all reader placements is rendered as a heatmap overlay, with density shown by color intensity rather than individual dots to preserve a sense of landscape rather than a vote count.

Reader Experience
  • Reader sees the blank plane with labeled axes after finishing the article.
  • A single tap or click places their marker. No sliders, no steps, no form.
  • The community heatmap fades in behind their marker after placement.
  • A brief interpretive sentence describes their position in plain language.
  • Optional: a before/after prompt if the delta mechanic is active.
Best-fit Article Types
Renewable energy Economic policy Urban development Technology regulation Any two-tension debate
Axis Design Rule

Axes must be genuinely independent. If moving along one axis predicts where someone sits on the other, the tool is redundant. The AI and editors confirm independence before publishing the map.

Strengths and Limits
Strengths
  • +Lowest cognitive load of all four tools. One gesture, done.
  • +Works on mobile without modification.
  • +Heatmap reveals polarization and clustering intuitively.
  • +Readers grasp the mechanic without instruction.
  • +Easy for editors to define well-structured axes.
Limits
  • -Debates with three genuinely distinct positions are compressed into two dimensions, which can misrepresent the actual structure.
  • -Axes that are not truly independent produce a meaningless diagonal cluster.
  • -Cannot express nuance within a quadrant.
Build Status
Prototype complete in dialecta-s02-ux-opinion-maps.jsx. Ready for integration testing against live article content. Axis-definition workflow with the editorial team is the outstanding design question before this ships.
Tool 02 of 04

The Ternary Plot

The signature feature. Three poles, one triangle, one gestalt placement that no other public platform currently offers.

Signature Feature
Tool 02
Ternary Plot
A triangular space where position is a blend of three simultaneous forces, not a choice between them.
How It Works

The triangle defines three poles. A point anywhere inside it represents a simultaneous blend of all three: moving toward one pole automatically reduces the weight of the other two, because all three values must sum to 100%. The reader makes a single gestalt placement. The community density map that builds up behind individual placements is the most revealing output: it shows not just where most people stand but whether the community clusters, splits, or spreads.

Why This Is the Signature Tool
  • No mainstream platform currently uses ternary plots for reader opinion mapping. The mechanic is academically established in political science and economics but has never been made accessible to general audiences.
  • The forced trade-off (moving toward one pole pulls from the others) is philosophically honest. Real positions on complex questions involve genuine tension between competing values. The ternary makes that tension explicit.
  • A density split into two camps inside the triangle tells a richer story than a split on a 2-axis plot, because it reveals which pair of poles people are caught between.
  • The geometry is unfamiliar enough to feel like a genuine discovery for most readers, which creates engagement.
Best-fit Article Types
Mental health Criminal justice Social media causes Education reform Any three-force debate
Strengths and Limits
Strengths
  • +Genuinely novel in public discourse. A real differentiator.
  • +Single placement, same cognitive simplicity as the 2-axis tool.
  • +Forced trade-off models real opinion structure more honestly than independent sliders.
  • +Density map is visually striking and immediately legible.
  • +Has academic credibility if the platform ever attracts press or research attention.
Limits
  • -Triangle geometry is unfamiliar to most readers and requires brief onboarding.
  • -The forced trade-off can frustrate readers who genuinely believe all three poles are equally important. (That position maps to the center, which is valid, but not all readers know that.)
  • -Mobile touch precision matters more here than in the 2-axis tool because the geometry is tighter.
  • -Only fits debates that genuinely have three competing poles, not two or four.
Build Status
Prototype complete in dialecta-s02-ux-opinion-maps.jsx. Onboarding copy for first-time users is the outstanding design question. The triangle placement behavior is proven; the interpretive text that follows needs calibration against real user placements.
Tool 03 of 04

The Radar / Spider Chart

A multi-dimensional shape that maps worldview across five to seven independent axes. High information density, higher cognitive cost. Works in a specific context that neither of the first two tools covers.

Conditional Use
Tool 03
Radar / Spider Chart
Five to seven independent axes. The reader's values become a shape. The community's values become a second shape laid over it.
How It Works

Each axis radiates outward from the center like a spoke on a wheel. The reader adjusts a slider for each axis, setting their position from 0 to 10. The resulting polygon is their shape. The community average polygon is overlaid semi-transparently. The reader can see where their shape is larger or smaller than the community's, revealing where their weighting differs from the group.

The Core Problem to Resolve First

The axes in the original brief were evaluation dimensions, not opinion dimensions. "Evidence quality" and "Moral weight" ask the reader to rate the article, not to locate their own position in the debate. That is a fundamentally different task, and it produces a review, not a map.

For the radar chart to function as an opinion map, every axis must be a genuine stance spectrum where a reader's position reflects their values, not their assessment of the argument's quality.

Example redesign for a criminal justice article: Rehabilitation emphasis vs. punitive emphasis. Individual accountability vs. systemic cause. Police investment level. Incarceration as success metric. Community-led intervention priority. These are positions, not ratings.

Where This Tool Fits

Not as a per-article default. The radar chart works best as a recurring reader values survey tied to a longer editorial series, or as a worldview self-portrait that contributors build up over time. It is closer to the Contributor Identity layer than to a comment-section tool.

Strengths and Limits
Strengths
  • +Reveals multi-dimensional worldview in a single visual.
  • +Personal vs. community shape comparison is genuinely interesting and can produce the "I didn't expect that" moment.
  • +Axes are independent, so the tool can handle debates that resist compression into two or three poles.
  • +Ties naturally into contributor identity: a reader's shape over time becomes part of their fingerprint.
Limits
  • -Seven sliders feels like a form, not reflection. The cognitive mode is wrong for a post-article interaction.
  • -The community average polygon conceals polarization. Two communities with opposite views on every axis can produce the same average shape as a moderate community.
  • -Near-unusable on mobile in a slider-per-axis configuration.
  • -Axis design is hard to get right. Poorly chosen axes produce uninformative shapes that cluster in the middle.
  • -The shape metaphor is less intuitive than a dot on a plane for most readers.
Recommended Path
Defer as a per-article tool. Revisit as a recurring reader values survey attached to editorial series, or as a periodic worldview snapshot that feeds into the Contributor Identity layer. Before building, the axis-design methodology needs to be established: specifically, a clear rule distinguishing opinion axes from evaluation axes.

Watch: The most common failure mode for this tool is axes that ask readers to rate rather than locate themselves. "How much evidence does this article use?" is a rating. "How much do you weigh empirical evidence vs. lived experience in forming your view on this?" is a position. The tool only works if every axis is the second kind.

Tool 04 of 04

Barycentric Multi-Pole

A ternary plot extended to four or more poles. Theoretically the most accurate representation of complex debates. In practice, not a reader-facing tool. Reconceived here as an editorial analytics layer.

Editorial Layer Only
Tool 04
Barycentric Multi-Pole
Four or more poles mapped in a tetrahedron and beyond. The geometry is correct. The reader experience is not viable.
The Geometry

With three poles, the ternary plot renders perfectly in 2D: a triangle is inherently flat. With four poles, the mathematical space is a tetrahedron, which must be projected onto a flat screen. That projection introduces distortion: points that are equidistant in the actual four-dimensional space appear at different distances in the 2D rendering. A reader cannot intuit this, and no amount of onboarding copy resolves it. The geometry is correct. The interface is not viable.

What It Should Actually Be

The right reconception is not a reader-facing interactive. It is an editorial analytics tool that answers a specific question before an article is published: does the debate actually have four distinct positions, or can it be honestly compressed into three?

The editorial team, with AI assistance, plots anticipated reader positions in four-dimensional barycentric space. If the projection reveals that positions naturally cluster into three groups despite four theoretical poles, they run a ternary plot. If all four poles are genuinely occupied, they consider an alternative: two separate ternary plots, each covering three of the four poles from different angles.

Two ternary plots is almost always a better solution than one four-pole projection. It preserves the reader experience while representing a genuinely complex debate honestly.

Genuine Value as a Backstage Tool
  • Reveals when a debate's actual structure does not match its surface framing.
  • Helps editors decide which mapping tool to deploy before publishing.
  • Over time, builds a picture of community opinion topology across the full archive.
  • Useful for editorial research and long-term platform analytics.
Strengths and Limits
Strengths (as editorial tool)
  • +Theoretically the most accurate representation of genuinely multi-polar debates.
  • +As a backstage tool, the rendering problem is editorial's problem, not the reader's.
  • +Useful for deciding which reader-facing tool to deploy.
  • +Produces long-term analytical value from reader placement data.
Limits
  • -Cannot be rendered in 2D without distortion artifacts that readers cannot interpret.
  • -No viable reader interaction model exists for four or more poles in a flat interface.
  • -Implementation complexity is significantly higher than the other three tools.
  • -The "power users only" framing from the original brief still applies, but it is better defined as editorial and research staff rather than advanced readers.
Recommended Path
Do not build as a reader-facing feature. Flag for the editorial dashboard in Phase 4, where it serves as a pre-publication decision aid and a long-term analytics layer. The implementation priority is low. The conceptual clarity about what it is and is not does the most important work right now.
Decision Record

Recommendation and Build Sequence

Where each tool fits in the roadmap and what needs to be resolved before the next one is built.

Tool Reader-Facing Phase Outstanding Question
2-Axis Cartesian Yes Phase 2, launch default Axis-definition workflow with editorial team. Who sets the axes, and what is the sign-off process before an article publishes?
Ternary Plot Yes Phase 3, beta alongside 2-Axis First-time user onboarding copy. Readers unfamiliar with ternary geometry need one sentence that makes the mechanic click before they place their marker.
Radar / Spider Chart Specific contexts only Phase 4, series-attached or identity layer Axis-design methodology: a clear rule distinguishing position axes from evaluation axes, and a decision on whether this is a per-article tool or a recurring reader values instrument tied to editorial series.
Barycentric Multi-Pole No (editorial layer only) Phase 4, backstage dashboard only Reconceived as an editorial pre-publication decision aid and long-term analytics layer. Reader-facing implementation is not viable. No build priority until editorial dashboard is scoped.

The right sequence: Ship the 2-axis and ternary tools, gather real user placement data, and let that data drive decisions about the radar chart and barycentric layer. The most valuable thing those two tools can do right now is generate the empirical evidence about how readers actually engage with spatial opinion mapping. That evidence will answer the design questions for tools 3 and 4 better than any amount of prior reasoning.

One additional principle worth recording: the framing around every tool must feel like reflection, not polling. The difference is in the copy, the pacing, and the interpretive text that follows placement. A tool that says "247 readers have mapped this" and then reveals a density cluster is doing something the internet does not currently offer. A tool that says "agree or disagree on five dimensions" is a survey. The mechanics are almost identical. The intention is not.
